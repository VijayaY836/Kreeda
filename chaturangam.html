How to play Chaturangam

Sixty seconds and you can play

  • Each side has 16 pieces: a Raja, a Mantri, two Gajas, two Ashvas, two Rathas and eight Padatis.
  • Ivory moves first. Tap your piece, then tap a highlighted square.
  • Landing on an enemy piece captures it. You may never leave your own Raja under attack.
  • Trap the enemy Raja so it cannot escape — that's checkmate, and you win.
If you already play chess, three things will trip you up: the Mantri moves one square diagonally (it is not a queen), the Gaja jumps exactly two squares diagonally (it is not a bishop), and Padatis never advance two squares. There is no castling.

Four limbs of an army

Chatur-anga means "four limbs" — the four divisions of a Gupta-era army: foot soldiers, horses, war elephants and chariots, all serving a king and his counsellor. The game turns that battle order into a board.

It appears in Indian texts from around the 6th century CE. From India it travelled west into Persia as Shatranj — where the cry shah mat, "the king is helpless", became our word checkmate — then through the Islamic world into Spain and Italy, where 15th-century players gave the queen and bishop their modern powers and chess as we know it was born. A separate branch went east and became Xiangqi and Shogi.

In Telugu it is చతురంగం (Chaturangam); you will also hear Chadarangam. The two-player version on this board is the one scholars reconstruct from Persian and Arabic sources — a four-player dice version, chaturaji, also existed.

An honest note

No complete rulebook survives from Gupta India. The moves here follow the standard reconstruction — the same one Shatranj used, which is the closest thing to direct evidence we have. The Gaja is the piece historians argue about most; some regional versions moved it like a modern rook instead.

Ashtapada, eight by eight

Chaturanga is played on the ashtapada — an eight-by-eight grid that already existed in India as a race-game board. It was uncheckered: plain lines, no alternating colours. Checkering came later, in Europe. You can switch between the two under Controls; the game plays identically.

Setting up

  • Back rank, left to right: Ratha, Ashva, Gaja, Raja, Mantri, Gaja, Ashva, Ratha.
  • Eight Padatis stand in front of them.
  • Both armies mirror each other, so Raja faces Raja on the d-file and Mantri faces Mantri on the e-file. This is not the modern chess setup, where the queens face each other on their own colour.
Squares are named the modern way — files a to h left to right, ranks 1 to 8 from the ivory side — purely so the move record is readable.

The six pieces

Pick a piece to see how it moves. Dots are moves, rings are captures, faint dots mark squares a Gaja leaps over.

Rules of play

Taking turns

  • Ivory moves first, then the sides alternate. You must move on your turn — passing is not allowed.
  • Move one piece per turn. Land on an enemy piece to capture it and take its square.
  • You cannot capture your own pieces, and only the Ashva and Gaja may pass over an occupied square.

Your Raja's safety

  • When a piece attacks your Raja, you are in check. Your next move must end the check — move the Raja, block the line, or capture the attacker.
  • Any move that would leave your own Raja attacked is illegal; the board will not let you play it.
  • There is no castling. The Raja walks.

Padati promotion

  • A Padati that reaches the far rank is promoted — but only ever to a Mantri. There is no choice of piece, and no second queen to dream about.
  • Since a Mantri moves a single diagonal step, promotion is a modest reward. It is worth roughly two Padatis, not nine.

What does not exist here

  • No two-square first move for Padatis, and therefore no en passant.
  • No castling, no queen, no bishop.

Three ways to win

1. Checkmate

The enemy Raja is attacked and has no legal way out. This is the main victory, exactly as in chess.

2. Stalemate — and here it is a win

If your opponent has no legal move at all but is not in check, you win. Modern chess calls that a draw; Shatranj scored it as a victory for the player who caused it, and this board follows the older rule. It rewards squeezing an opponent dry.

3. Baring the Raja

Capture every enemy piece so that only the Raja stands alone, and you win. One exception: if your opponent can immediately bare your Raja on their reply, the game is drawn instead.

These last two rules matter more than they sound. With no queen on the board, endgames are slow and material grinds down — baring the Raja is a real finish, not a curiosity.

The practical effect: the game is much slower. Long-range attacks barely exist, so plans are built a square at a time and a single Ratha becomes enormously powerful.

Tactics that actually work here

  • Rathas decide games. They are the only long-range piece. Open a file for one and it will dominate a board where nothing else can answer from a distance.
  • Ashvas outrank Gajas. A Gaja can only ever reach eight squares on the whole board and can never change that set — two Gajas on the same colour are nearly redundant. Trade a Gaja for an Ashva whenever you're offered it.
  • Padati chains are strong. With no two-square jump, both sides advance slowly, so a wall of mutually defending Padatis is hard to break.
  • Walk the Raja out early. There is no castling and no queen hunting it. A centralised Raja is a genuine fighting piece here, not a liability.
  • Count for the bare-Raja finish. If you are up material and cannot force mate, hunting the last enemy piece is a legitimate winning plan.
